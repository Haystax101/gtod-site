## In one line

There are four interview types you will meet in UK apprenticeship recruitment — competency, strengths, values and motivational/commercial — and each needs a different kind of answer, so the first job in any interview is working out which one you are in.[^i1][^i4][^i12][^i18]

## Key facts

- A competency (or behavioural) interview asks what you actually did in a past situation, and the standard published answer structure is STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result.[^i1]
- The National Careers Service and GOV.UK guidance both say to keep the situation and task brief and spend most of the answer on the action you personally took and the result.[^i1][^i2]
- Some UK government guidance uses STARR, adding Reflection, or CAR (Context, Action, Result) as alternatives to plain STAR.[^i3]
- A strengths interview asks what you enjoy and what energises you; Civil Service guidance says strengths answers are short, typically no more than two minutes, and explicitly tells candidates not to rehearse.[^i4][^i20]
- The Civil Service defines a strength as needing all three of performance (you do it well), engagement (it motivates you), and use (you do it often).[^i4]
- The Civil Service publishes a Strengths Dictionary listing the strengths it looks for, and states no candidate is expected to demonstrate all of them for one job.[^i4][^i8]
- Employers reported as using strengths-based rather than competency questions at interview include EY, Barclays, Aviva, Nestlé and Standard Chartered.[^i9]
- NHS values-based recruitment selects on alignment with the values of the NHS Constitution, alongside the usual assessment of aptitude and skills, and can be delivered through structured interviews, multiple mini interviews, role play, written scenarios and assessment centres.[^i12][^i13][^i14]
- Commercial awareness means knowing how the employer makes money, who its customers and competitors are, and what external forces affect it.[^i18][^i19]

## What is a competency interview and how do I answer one?

A competency interview, also called a behavioural interview, asks you to describe something you have actually done. The underlying logic is that past behaviour predicts future behaviour, and CIPD lists structured behavioural interviewing among the standard selection methods employers use.[^i24]

The published answer structure is STAR:[^i1]

- **Situation** — the context, in one or two sentences.
- **Task** — what specifically you were responsible for.
- **Action** — what you personally did, step by step.
- **Result** — what changed because of it, with a number where you have one.

Published UK government guidance adds three things worth taking seriously:

1. **Weight the answer towards Action and Result.** Keep the situation and task short: what matters is what you did and what happened.[^i1][^i2]
2. **Say "I", not "we".** If it was a team effort, say so, then be explicit about your own part in it.[^i1][^i2]
3. **Do not announce the letters.** Tell it as a story rather than reciting "so, my situation was...".[^i1]

Some UK public bodies use **STARR**, adding a Reflection step on what you learned or would do differently, or **CAR** (Context, Action, Result) as a shorter form.[^i3] If the employer names a variant, use theirs.

**GTOD take:** at 17 or 18 you are allowed to use school, sports, part-time work, gaming clans, caring responsibilities, a Duke of Edinburgh expedition and a Saturday job as your examples. A competency interview is testing the shape of your judgement, not the prestige of your CV. What kills answers at this age is vagueness, not lack of a corporate example.

## What is a strengths-based interview and how is it different?

A strengths interview asks what you are good at and enjoy, not what you have previously done. LSE Careers describes the approach as exploring innate strengths and what a candidate enjoys rather than past behaviour, and argues it is fairer and more inclusive because it selects on natural talent rather than on who happened to have particular past experiences.[^i9]

The Civil Service publishes the clearest definition. Something counts as a strength only if all three of these are true:[^i4]

- **Performance** — you can do it to a high level.
- **Engagement** — doing it motivates and energises you.
- **Use** — you do it regularly and often.

### What does a strengths interview feel like?

- **Lots of questions, answered quickly.** Civil Service guidance says strengths questions need short answers, typically two minutes at most.[^i20]
- **Some questions feel repetitive.** That is deliberate: repetition lets the interviewer check whether your answers are consistent.[^i9]
- **There are no right or wrong answers**, and the Civil Service says so in terms.[^i20]
- **You are told not to rehearse.** Both the strengths interview guidance and the Civil Service Work Strengths Test guidance say the assessment is looking for your initial response, and that the useful preparation is reflecting on your genuine strengths and preferred ways of working rather than scripting.[^i7][^i20]

The Civil Service Work Strengths Test is the online sibling of this interview: an untimed questionnaire scored on your pattern of responses, reported as a percentile against a comparison group, with passing scores banked and reused for future applications at the same grade grouping.[^i7]

**GTOD take:** the honest strategy is the winning strategy here, and not for moral reasons. A strengths interview is designed to catch inconsistency across twenty-odd fast questions. You cannot hold a fabricated profile steady that long, and interviewers are trained to notice when the energy drops on an answer you invented.

## What is a values-based interview and where is it used?

A values-based interview tests whether your values and behaviours match the organisation's published values. It is the dominant approach across the NHS and is widely used in the wider public sector and in charities.

NHS values-based recruitment selects students, trainees and employees on the basis that their individual values and behaviours align with the values of the NHS Constitution, as part of a process that also assesses aptitude and skills.[^i12] The national framework was published in 2014 and refreshed in 2016.[^i13] The NHS Constitution sets out six values expected of all NHS staff: working together for patients, respect and dignity, commitment to quality of care, compassion, improving lives, and everyone counts.[^i15] These map onto the 6Cs — care, compassion, competence, communication, courage and commitment.[^i17]

NHS values-based recruitment can be delivered through pre-screening assessments, values-based interview technique, role play, written responses to scenarios and assessment centre methods.[^i12][^i13] NHS guidance states that an individual structured interview, which may take the form of multiple mini interviews, must be part of the process where values are being assessed.[^i14] NHS Employers also publishes a behaviour framework that can be used directly as interview scoring criteria.[^i12]

Health Careers advice for candidates is to think about how you demonstrate the values, drawing on work experience, volunteering or personal life such as helping family or friends.[^i15][^i16]

**GTOD take:** for an NHS apprenticeship, read the NHS Constitution values before you do anything else and prepare one concrete story per value. Panels ask about them directly — "what do you know about the NHS Constitution and its values" is on NHS England's own published list of common questions.[^i16]

## What are motivational and commercial-awareness questions testing?

**Motivational questions** ask why this job, why this employer, why an apprenticeship rather than a degree, and why now. Deloitte publishes a "skills and motivation interview" as an explicit part of its early-careers final stage.[^i22] PwC opens its process with a non-assessed values activity designed to help you judge whether its culture suits you, then assesses fit later.[^i23]

**Commercial awareness questions** test whether you understand the business. Published definitions describe it as knowledge of the business you work in, the sectors it works with, your employer, its competitors and the key issues facing them.[^i18] ICAEW frames commercial awareness as an employability skill in its own right.[^i19] targetjobs breaks it into three components: understanding the business and others like it and what makes it successful; knowing the marketplace and who the business interacts with such as clients, regulators and investors; and being able to identify competitors and the factors affecting business performance.[^i18]

**GTOD take:** commercial awareness for a school leaver does not mean quoting the employer's revenue. It means being able to answer three questions in your own words: how does this company make money, who else does what they do, and what changed for them in the last year. If you can do that plus name one thing you read about their industry this month, you are ahead of most applicants.

## Practice question bank

The questions below are grouped by interview type. Except where marked as published by an employer or public body, they are questions we have composed for practice. Use them to rehearse *material*, not scripts — especially for the strengths section, where rehearsal is explicitly counterproductive.[^i7][^i20]

### Competency questions (answer with STAR)

**Teamwork and working with others**

1. Tell me about a time you worked in a team where someone was not pulling their weight. What did you do?
2. Describe a time you had to work with someone whose approach was completely different from yours.
3. Give me an example of a time you disagreed with a decision your group made. How did you handle it?
4. Tell me about a time you helped someone else succeed at something.
5. Describe a situation where you had to build a relationship with someone quickly.

**Communication and influence**

6. Tell me about a time you had to explain something complicated to someone who did not know the subject.
7. Describe a time you had to persuade someone to change their mind.
8. Give an example of when you had to deliver difficult or unwelcome news.
9. Tell me about a piece of writing you produced that had to be right first time.
10. Describe a time you noticed someone had misunderstood you. What did you do?

**Problem solving and analysis**

11. Tell me about a problem you solved where the obvious answer turned out to be wrong.
12. Describe a time you had to make a decision without all the information you wanted.
13. Give an example of when you spotted a mistake nobody else had noticed.
14. Tell me about a time you had to work with numbers or data to reach a conclusion.
15. Describe the most complicated thing you have had to plan.

**Resilience, pressure and setbacks**

16. Tell me about a time you failed at something that mattered to you.
17. Describe a time when you were under significant time pressure. How did you cope?
18. Give an example of when you received criticism you did not agree with.
19. Tell me about a time you had to keep going with something you found boring or repetitive.
20. Describe a time you had to change your plan at short notice.

**Initiative, ownership and learning**

21. Tell me about something you started that nobody asked you to do.
22. Describe a time you taught yourself a skill from scratch.
23. Give an example of when you took responsibility for something going wrong.
24. Tell me about a time you improved how something was done.
25. Describe the last thing you learned that changed how you do something.

**Customer, client and service**

26. Tell me about a time you dealt with someone who was upset or angry.
27. Describe a time you went beyond what was asked of you for someone else.
28. Give an example of when you had to say no to a request.

**Integrity and judgement**

29. Tell me about a time you saw something being done that you thought was wrong.
30. Describe a situation where doing the right thing was inconvenient.
31. Give an example of when you had to keep something confidential.

### Strengths questions (answer fast and honestly, roughly two minutes maximum)

Two of these are published by the Civil Service as examples of the strengths questions it asks:[^i20]

32. What motivates you to get up in the morning? *(published Civil Service example)*[^i20]
33. Would others describe you as an analytical person? *(published Civil Service example)*[^i20]

The rest are ours:

34. What kind of task makes you lose track of time?
35. What do people come to you for help with?
36. When in the last month did you feel most effective?
37. Do you prefer starting things or finishing them?
38. What part of school or work drains you fastest?
39. Are you better at spotting problems or fixing them?
40. What would you happily do all day if someone paid you to?
41. When you get a new piece of software or equipment, do you read the instructions or start pressing things?
42. Do you like being given a method, or working one out?
43. What is something you are good at that you do not particularly enjoy?
44. Do you get more energy from working alone or with other people?
45. What is the last thing you did purely because you found it interesting?
46. Would your friends describe you as organised?
47. How do you react when a plan changes at the last minute?
48. What do you do when you are stuck?
49. Do you prefer detail or the big picture?
50. What is a compliment you have had more than once?
51. When have you felt out of your depth, and what did you do about it?
52. What do you do that other people find tedious but you do not?

LSE Careers notes that strengths interviews often still include a weakness question and advises answering it honestly.[^i9] So:

53. What is something you find genuinely difficult?

### Values questions (NHS and public sector)

Some of these are on NHS England's published list of common interview questions for allied health professional roles:[^i16]

54. What do you know about the NHS Constitution and its values? *(published NHS England example)*[^i16]
55. Why do you want to work for the NHS / this organisation? *(published NHS England example)*[^i16]
56. What would you do if you saw a senior colleague make a mistake? *(published NHS England example)*[^i16]
57. Describe a time when you received constructive criticism. *(published NHS England example)*[^i16]
58. How do you promote equality and diversity in your practice? *(published NHS England example)*[^i16]
59. What are your strengths and areas for development? *(published NHS England example)*[^i16]

Ours, mapped to the NHS Constitution values and the 6Cs:[^i15][^i17]

60. Tell me about a time you treated someone with dignity when it would have been easier not to.
61. Describe a situation where you put someone else's needs ahead of your own convenience.
62. Give an example of when you spoke up about something that was not right. *(courage)*
63. Tell me about a time you had to be compassionate to someone you found difficult.
64. Describe a time when you improved something for the people you were serving.
65. How would you handle a colleague who was rude to a patient or a member of the public?
66. What does "everyone counts" mean to you in a workplace?
67. Tell me about a time you kept a commitment when circumstances made it hard.
68. Describe how you would handle being asked to do something you did not feel competent to do.

For non-NHS public sector, map instead to the employer's published framework — for the Civil Service that means the Success Profiles behaviours for the grade you are applying to.[^i5][^i6]

### Motivational questions

69. Why an apprenticeship rather than a full-time degree?
70. Why this employer specifically, rather than its three closest competitors?
71. What made you choose this industry at 18 rather than waiting?
72. What do you think a first-year apprentice in this role actually does day to day?
73. What will be hardest for you about combining study and full-time work?
74. Where do you want to be five years after finishing the apprenticeship?
75. What would make you leave this apprenticeship?
76. What have you done to find out what this job is really like?
77. Which part of this scheme are you least looking forward to?
78. What do you want from us that you could not get somewhere else?

### Commercial awareness questions

79. How does this company make money?
80. Who are our main competitors, and what do they do differently?
81. What business story or deal have you found most interesting recently? *(a question form named in published careers guidance)*[^i18]
82. What is the biggest external risk facing this industry in the next three years?
83. If you had to cut 10% of costs here, where would you look first, and what would you be careful about?
84. What effect do you think AI will have on this profession in the next five years?
85. How does regulation affect what this business can do?
86. Who are our customers, and what would make one of them leave us?
87. What news story from the last month is relevant to us, and why?
88. What would you say is the difference between us and a smaller firm doing similar work?

### Questions to ask them

89. What does a successful first six months look like for an apprentice here?
90. How much of the off-the-job training is protected, and what happens when work gets busy?
91. Who will actually manage me, and who will mentor me?
92. What proportion of your apprentices completed and stayed on last year?
93. What is the most common reason an apprentice struggles here?

**GTOD take:** question 92 is the single most revealing thing you can ask, and very few candidates ask it. An employer that knows its completion and retention numbers and answers straightforwardly is running a real programme. Vagueness there is worth noticing.

## How do I tell which interview type I am in?

- If the questions start "tell me about a time when", you are in a **competency** interview. Use STAR.[^i1]
- If they come fast, feel personal, and there are many of them, you are in a **strengths** interview. Answer in under two minutes, honestly, first thought.[^i20]
- If the employer keeps referring to its published values or the NHS Constitution, you are in a **values** interview. Anchor every answer to a named value.[^i12][^i15]
- If they ask about the business, the market or the news, you are in **commercial awareness**. Show that you understand how they make money.[^i18][^i19]

Most real apprenticeship interviews are mixed. Civil Service guidance describes interviews that combine behaviour questions with strengths questions in the same session.[^i20][^i21] Deloitte's final stage combines a prepared-topic discussion with a skills and motivation interview.[^i22]
