## In one line

The official data does not crown a winner: graduates earn a median £33,300 five years out (2023-24 tax year) and are far more likely to be in high-skilled work, but Level 4 and 5 apprentices earned more five years after finishing (£38,630 and £35,250 in the same tax year), carry no student debt, and are older on average, so the two groups are not like for like.[^s3][^s1]

## Key facts

- First degree graduates in England had median annualised earnings of £33,300 five years after graduating, measured in the 2023-24 tax year (published 25 June 2026); 88.3% were in sustained employment, further study or both.[^s3][^s4]
- Apprentices who finished in 2018/19 earned, five years later (2023-24 tax year), a median £26,810 (Level 2 intermediate), £29,330 (Level 3 advanced), £38,630 (Level 4) and £35,250 (Level 5); the release was published 18 December 2025.[^s1]
- One year after finishing, 2022/23 apprentices earned a median £24,070 (Level 2), £26,920 (Level 3), £33,760 (Level 4), £37,570 (Level 5), £34,610 (Level 6) and £49,930 (Level 7 and above).[^s1]
- 94% of apprentices who completed in 2022/23 were in sustained employment a year later, compared with 78% of other further education learners.[^s1]
- DfE's LEO comparison of people ten years after their GCSEs (2012-13 GCSE cohort, 2023-24 tax year) finds graduates usually earn more than people with Level 4/5 or Level 3 qualifications, but that Level 4/5 apprenticeships offer the highest returns at that age, and men with STEM Level 4/5 qualifications out-earn STEM graduates at most prior-attainment levels.[^s2]
- IFS (25 June 2026) estimates the average graduate ends up about £100,000 (15%) better off over a lifetime after tax and loan repayments: £90,000 for women, £109,000 for men, roughly 30% lower than its 2020 estimate.[^s9]
- The same IFS study finds about 20% of women and 30% of men who go to university would be financially better off not going, and returns for medicine and economics exceed £400,000 while creative arts, philosophy and languages average low or negative.[^s9]
- Working-age graduates (16 to 64) had an employment rate of 87.6% and a median salary of £42,000 in 2024, against 68.0% and £30,500 for non-graduates; 67.9% of graduates were in high-skilled jobs versus 23.7% of non-graduates.[^s10]
- The national apprenticeship achievement rate was 65.4% in 2024/25 (up from 60.5% in 2023/24); the retention rate was 66.7%, meaning about a third of apprentices did not stay to the end.[^s5][^s7]
- Level 6 (degree-level) apprenticeships had the highest achievement rate at 69.3% in 2024/25; Level 4 had the lowest at 60.9%.[^s5][^s7]
- The OfS sector-wide continuation rate for full-time first degree students was 91.1% and the completion rate 89.4% in the data used to set its 2022 thresholds; its minimum thresholds are 80% continuation and 75% completion for full-time first degrees, but only 70% and 55% for full-time undergraduate apprenticeships.[^s16]
- As at 3 September 2026, English students who started a course on or after 1 August 2023 are on Plan 5: repay 9% of income above £25,000 a year, written off 40 years after the April you were first due to repay; GOV.UK showed the Plan 5 interest rate as 4.1%.[^s11][^s14][^s15]
- The maximum tuition fee loan for 2026 to 2027 is £9,790, up from £9,535 in 2025 to 2026.[^s13]
- In 2024/25 there were 353,500 apprenticeship starts in England; 51.3% were by people aged 25 and over and only 21.2% by under-19s; Level 6 and 7 starts were 60,350 (17.1% of all starts).[^s5]
- Sutton Trust (December 2022) found that over half of degree apprentices were aged over 25 and only 5% had been eligible for free school meals, against 6.7% of university entrants.[^s18]
- ISE's 2025 Student Recruitment Survey found graduate vacancies fell 8%; ISE said on 25 August 2026 that Adzuna's reported 45% year-on-year fall in advertised graduate vacancies overstates the drop in actual hiring.[^s20]

## Do graduates or apprentices earn more?

It depends on the level of the apprenticeship, the subject of the degree, and when you measure. All the DfE figures below come from the Longitudinal Education Outcomes (LEO) dataset, which links education records to HMRC tax records, and all are medians for people in sustained employment.[^s1][^s2]

For graduates: the median first degree graduate earned £33,300 five years after graduating, measured in the 2023-24 tax year.[^s3] That is a real-terms rise of 0.4% since 2017-18 and 2.3% since 2022-23.[^s3] 88.3% were in sustained employment, further study or both.[^s3]

For apprentices, five years after finishing (2018/19 completers, also measured in 2023-24): Level 2 £26,810, Level 3 £29,330, Level 4 £38,630, Level 5 £35,250.[^s1] Five-year figures for Level 6 and 7 apprentices are not yet published because those levels are too new.[^s1]

So in the same tax year, Level 4 and Level 5 apprentices out-earned the median graduate five years on, while Level 2 and 3 apprentices earned less.[^s1][^s3]

**GTOD take:** the gap in favour of Level 4 and 5 apprentices is partly an age effect.[^s1][^s5] Over half of apprenticeship starts are by people aged 25 and over, so "five years after finishing" for an apprentice is often someone in their thirties, while a graduate five years out is typically about 26 or 27.[^s5] Neither dataset records hours worked, so part-time work drags down the graduate medians, especially for women.[^s1][^s2]

## What do apprentices earn straight after finishing?

One year after completing in 2022/23 (measured in the 2023-24 tax year), median earnings were: Level 2 intermediate £24,070; Level 3 advanced £26,920; Level 4 £33,760; Level 5 £37,570; Level 6 £34,610; Level 7 and above £49,930.[^s1]

**GTOD take:** the Level 7 figure of £49,930 is not a school-leaver salary.[^s1] Level 7 apprenticeships (master's level) are mostly taken by people already in professional jobs, and 51.3% of all starts are by people aged 25 and over.[^s1][^s5]

Earnings growth after an apprenticeship is strong. For the 2018/19 cohort, Level 2 median earnings rose 49% between one and five years after finishing (£18,050 to £26,810), Level 3 rose 42% (£20,700 to £29,330), Level 4 rose 50% (£25,780 to £38,630) and Level 5 rose 22% (£28,850 to £35,250).[^s1]

94% of 2022/23 apprentices were in sustained employment a year later, the highest of any further education route; Level 4, 5 and 6 apprentices were at 95% and Level 2 at 92%.[^s1]

> The Further education outcomes release also publishes earnings three years after achievement, but I could not extract the three-year figures by level from the page. The underlying data tables in the release would settle them.

## What is the graduate premium in England now?

DfE's new LEO comparison follows everyone who sat GCSEs in 2012-13 to ten years later (the 2023-24 tax year) and compares people with the same sex and GCSE grades.[^s2][^s23]

People with no higher-level qualification earned £18,300 to £25,600 (women) and £24,500 to £34,000 (men), depending on their GCSE attainment quintile.[^s2]

Compared with those non-graduates, female STEM graduates earned £8,700 to £12,800 more, and male STEM graduates £2,900 to £7,700 more.[^s2] Female LEM (law, economics, management) graduates earned £6,200 to £13,200 more and male LEM graduates £2,600 to £11,000 more.[^s2]

For "other" subjects the premium was small or absent: female graduates earned £4,000 to £5,500 more, while male graduates earned between £2,200 more and £700 less than similar non-graduates.[^s2]

The same release says graduates typically out-earn people with Level 4/5 non-apprenticeship qualifications, who in turn out-earn people with Level 3 or below.[^s2] But it also says Level 4/5 apprenticeships give the highest returns at that age, and that for men in STEM, Level 4/5 qualifications beat Level 6 at most prior-attainment levels ten years after GCSEs.[^s2]

The older DfE survey-based series (Labour Force Survey, calendar year 2024) gives the whole-workforce picture: working-age graduates earned a median £42,000 against £30,500 for non-graduates, and 67.9% of graduates were in high-skilled jobs against 23.7% of non-graduates.[^s10] That series was discontinued after the 2024 edition and replaced by the LEO release from June 2026.[^s10]

For 21 to 30 year olds in 2024, Universities UK summarised the same DfE data as 60% of graduates in high-skilled jobs against 21.5% of non-graduates, with unemployment at 5.5% for graduates and 8.1% for non-graduates.[^s19]

**GTOD take:** the "non-graduate" group in the survey figures includes everyone from people with no GCSEs to Level 5 apprentices, so a £42,000 versus £30,500 gap tells you little about a specific apprenticeship.[^s10] The LEO comparison by GCSE grades is the fairer test, and it shows the premium is real but heavily subject-dependent.[^s2][^s10]

## Does a degree pay off over a lifetime?

The Institute for Fiscal Studies, funded by DfE, published new estimates on 25 June 2026 using tax records for people born in the mid-1980s observed to age 37, then simulated to retirement under 2025/26 tax and Plan 5 loan rules.[^s9]

Before tax and loans, women who go to university earn about £150,000 (20%) more over their lives and men about £220,000 (20%) more than similar people who did not go.[^s9]

After tax and student loan repayments, the average graduate is about £100,000 (15%) better off: £90,000 for women and £109,000 for men.[^s9] The median is lower, at £67,000 for women and £60,000 for men, because a few high earners pull the average up.[^s9]

About 20% of women and 30% of men would have been financially better off not going.[^s9] Medicine and economics return over £400,000 on average; creative arts, philosophy and languages return little or lose money on average.[^s9]

For students in the bottom 35% by GCSE scores, the average return is still positive (around £72,000 for women, £36,000 for men), but roughly 40% of men in that group lose money.[^s9]

These figures are about 30% (£46,000) lower than the IFS 2020 study, which found net returns of £130,000 for men and £100,000 for women and that about one in five would have been better off not going.[^s8] The fall is because real earnings turned out lower than modelled, especially for women aged 30 to 35, and because less generous maintenance loans, changed loan terms and frozen tax thresholds cut net returns by around £15,000.[^s8][^s9]

**GTOD take:** the IFS work compares graduates with similar non-graduates in general, not with apprentices specifically, and IFS itself says it does not compare university with alternatives. Nobody has yet published a like-for-like lifetime comparison of a degree apprenticeship against a full-time degree.[^s9]

> I could not open any IFS study giving earnings returns to apprenticeships by level. ## How many apprentices drop out compared with university students?

Apprenticeships: the national achievement rate was 65.4% in 2024/25, up 4.9 percentage points from 60.5% in 2023/24, published in March 2026.[^s5] That is just short of the government's 67% target set in 2022.[^s7]

The retention rate was 66.7% in 2024/25 (up from 61.9%), so roughly one in three apprentices left before the end.[^s7] Achievement rose 5.9 percentage points in 2023/24 and again in 2024/25, so the trend is improving.[^s6][^s5]

**GTOD take:** because the achievement rate (65.4%) sits just below the retention rate (66.7%), almost everyone who stays to the end passes.[^s7] The problem is leaving early, not failing the final assessment.[^s7]

By level in 2024/25: Level 3 (advanced) 66.4%, Level 4 60.9% (the lowest for the third year running), Level 5 66.2%, Level 6 69.3%.[^s7] By age, 19 to 23 year olds did best at 67.3%, under-19s were at 64.3%.[^s7][^s5]

University: the OfS measures continuation (still studying or qualified one year and 15 days after starting) and completion (qualified or still studying four years and 15 days after starting).[^s17] The sector-wide rates for full-time first degree students in the data OfS used to set its 2022 thresholds were 91.1% continuation and 89.4% completion.[^s16]

OfS sets a minimum of 80% continuation and 75% completion for full-time first degree courses, but only 70% continuation and 55% completion for full-time undergraduate apprenticeships, which shows the regulator expects apprenticeship completion to be lower.[^s16]

**GTOD take:** the two measures are not the same. "Achievement" for an apprentice means passing the final assessment at the end of the whole programme, while the university 91.1% figure is about surviving the first year and the 89.4% figure counts people still studying after four years as well as those who finished.[^s16][^s17] Even so, a one in three drop-out rate is the single biggest risk on the apprenticeship side and you should ask any employer for their own completion figures.[^s7][^s16]

## What will university cost me in loans?

If you start a course in England on or after 1 August 2023 you are on Plan 5.[^s15] As at 3 September 2026 the maximum tuition fee loan is £9,790 a year for 2026 to 2027 (it was £9,535 for 2025 to 2026).[^s13]

Maintenance loans for 2026 to 2027 go up to £9,118 if you live with parents, £10,830 away from home outside London and £14,135 in London; how much you get depends on household income.[^s13]

Repayments are 9% of income above £25,000 a year (£2,083 a month).[^s11] Anything left is written off 40 years after the April you were first due to repay.[^s11][^s14]

Interest on Plan 5 is set each 1 September from the previous March's RPI, with a cap linked to commercial rates.[^s12] On 3 September 2026 GOV.UK's repayment page showed the Plan 5 rate as 4.1%.[^s11][^s12]

For comparison, Plan 2 (courses started between September 2012 and July 2023) has a threshold of £29,385, a 30-year write-off and interest currently capped at 6%.[^s11][^s14][^s15]

Apprentices are employees, earning a wage and holiday pay from the start.[^s24] Apprenticeship training is paid for by the employer and the government rather than the apprentice, so none of the loan figures above apply to an apprentice (see GTOD's separate document on financial support for apprentices).

**GTOD take:** Plan 5 is a 9% graduate tax on earnings above £25,000 for up to 40 years, which for most graduates means paying it for most of their working life.[^s11][^s14] IFS's 2026 figures already build that in, and the degree still comes out ahead on average.[^s9] But on a below-average graduate salary, the loan can wipe out most of the earnings premium.[^s9][^s11]

> [!GAP]
> The GOV.UK Plan 5 interest guidance page's rate table stopped at "1 September 2024 to 31 August 2025: 4.3%" when opened on 3 September 2026, while the repayment page showed 4.1%. I trust the repayment page as the live figure, but check the guidance page for the 2026-27 rate once updated.

## Who actually does degree apprenticeships, and do they finish?

In 2024/25 there were 353,500 apprenticeship starts in England, 4.1% more than 2023/24.[^s5] Level 4 and above made up 39.8% of starts.[^s5] Level 6 and 7 starts together were 60,350 (17.1% of all starts): 26,800 at Level 6 and 33,560 at Level 7.[^s5]

Only 21.2% of all starts were by under-19s (down from 23.2%), while 51.3% were by people aged 25 and over.[^s5]

Sutton Trust research (8 December 2022, covering 2015 to 2020) found that people over 25 made up the vast majority of higher apprentices and over half of degree apprentices, and that better-off areas had gained most from degree apprenticeship growth.[^s18] Only 5% of degree apprentices had been eligible for free school meals, compared with 6.7% of university entrants.[^s18]

Among apprentices under 30, the free-school-meals share falls as the level rises: 13% at Level 2, 9% at Level 3 and 7% at Level 4/5.[^s18]

On completion, Level 6 apprenticeships had the highest achievement rate of any level at 69.3% in 2024/25, and universities and other providers achieved 73%, higher than colleges (65.7%) or independent training providers (63.4%).[^s5][^s7]

One year after finishing in 2022/23, Level 6 apprentices earned a median £34,610, and 95% of Level 4 to 6 apprentices were in sustained employment.[^s1]

**GTOD take:** degree apprenticeships are not mainly a school-leaver route yet. Most places go to people already in work or aged 25 and over, and the social mix is narrower than university.[^s5][^s18] That does not make them a bad choice for an 18 year old, but it means competition for the small number of school-leaver places is fierce.[^s5][^s18]

> [!GAP]
> No published source I could open gives a completion rate or earnings figure specifically for degree apprentices who started straight from school at 18. DfE achievement rates by level and age exist in the national achievement rate tables but I could not open the age-by-level breakdown for Level 6.

## Is the graduate job market getting worse?

The Institute of Student Employers' 2025 Student Recruitment Survey found employers' graduate vacancies fell 8%.[^s20] Its 2026 survey is due on 14 October 2026.[^s20]

On 25 August 2026, ISE responded to Adzuna data showing a 45% year-on-year fall in advertised graduate vacancies.[^s20] ISE argued that advertised "graduate jobs" are not the same as all jobs open to graduates, that firms increasingly hire without advertising, and that the market is very competitive but not down by 45%.[^s20]

HESA's Graduate Outcomes survey of 2022/23 graduates, summarised by Universities UK in July 2025, found 59% in full-time work 15 months after graduating and unemployment of 6%, up from 5% the year before.[^s19]

> [!GAP]
> ISE's own survey reports are behind a paywall (£216.67 plus VAT for non-members), so I could not verify apprenticeship vacancy trends or starting salaries from ISE directly.

## Why should I be careful with all of these numbers?

Earnings data lags badly. The newest LEO figures are for the 2023-24 tax year, so "five years after graduating" describes people who left university in 2018 and finished school around 2015.[^s3][^s1] IFS's lifetime estimates are for people born in the mid-1980s.[^s9]

Apprentices and graduates are different people. Apprentices are older on average, with 51.3% of starts by over-25s, and many were already employed before starting.[^s5] Graduates have higher average GCSE and A level grades, which is why DfE's pathway comparison controls for GCSE attainment and the IFS controls for background.[^s2][^s9]

HMRC PAYE records do not include hours worked, so all LEO medians mix full-time and part-time workers and only count people in sustained employment earning at least £3,000 a year.[^s1][^s23]

Survey-based figures (the old DfE Graduate labour market statistics, ONS Labour Force Survey) are estimates with growing sampling error because survey response rates have fallen, and DfE badged its 2024 edition as official statistics in development.[^s10]

The last ONS "Graduates in the UK labour market" article was published on 24 November 2017 and found 49% of recent graduates in non-graduate jobs; it is too old to rely on for current pay.[^s21]

Medians hide spread. Within one subject, economics graduates' interquartile range five years out was £32,900, while sport and exercise science graduates' was £11,700.[^s2]

**GTOD take:** treat every figure in this document as "people like you a decade ago did roughly this", not as a forecast. The employer, the subject, the level and whether you finish matter far more than the route label.[^s9][^s1]

## So which should I choose?

**GTOD take:** the evidence points to three honest conclusions. First, a degree in a high-return subject (medicine, economics, law, most STEM) from almost any university still beats most alternatives on lifetime money, even after Plan 5 repayments.[^s9] Second, a Level 4 to 6 apprenticeship with a good employer matches or beats the median graduate on early-career pay with zero debt, but with roughly a one in three chance of not finishing.[^s1][^s7] Third, a degree in a low-return subject for a student with weak grades is the riskiest route financially: IFS finds around 40% of low-attainment men lose money on university.[^s9]

**GTOD take:** if you cannot get a strong apprenticeship offer, university is not a bad default. If you can, the data says the apprenticeship is at least as good financially, provided you finish.[^s1][^s9]
