## In one line

An apprentice in England is a normal employee with a signed apprenticeship agreement and training plan, and gets every statutory employment right (paid holiday, sick pay, working-hour limits, redundancy pay, protection from discrimination) plus paid off-the-job training, with funded support if you are made redundant.[^s4][^s2][^s22]

## Key facts

- The law treats an approved English apprenticeship agreement as a contract of service, so you are an employee with the same rights as any other employee.[^s4][^s20]
- Your employer must sign an apprenticeship agreement with you, and you, your employer and your training provider must all sign a separate training plan.[^s1]
- Since 1 August 2025 the practical (working and training) period of an apprenticeship must last at least 8 months; before that it was 12 months.[^s6][^s7]
- Almost all workers get 5.6 weeks of paid holiday a year, capped at 28 days; bank holidays do not have to be extra.[^s10]
- As at September 2026 (tax year 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027), statutory sick pay is £123.25 a week or 80% of your average weekly earnings, whichever is lower, paid from your first day off sick.[^s14][^s16]
- If you are under 18 you cannot work more than 8 hours a day or 40 hours a week, and you cannot opt out of that limit.[^s17]
- Under the 2026 to 2027 funding rules, every apprenticeship standard has a published minimum number of off-the-job training hours, which cannot be cut below 187 hours even after prior learning is counted.[^s7][^s8]
- Off-the-job training must happen in your normal paid working hours; training outside them only counts if you agree and get time off in lieu or extra pay.[^s8]
- If you are made redundant, the Department for Education keeps funding your training for at least 12 weeks; if you have under 6 months left or have done 75% or more, you can finish without a new employer.[^s22]
- Learning support funding for apprentices with a learning difficulty or disability is £150 a month, paid to your provider, not taken from your employer's account.[^s7]
- Complaints go to your provider's own procedure first; only then will the Department for Education look at them, and not if the issue is more than 12 months old.[^s30]
- The Apprentice Support Centre helpline is 08000 150 400 (nationalhelpdesk@apprenticeships.gov.uk).[^s31]
- From 1 January 2027 the Employment Rights Act 2025 is due to cut the qualifying period for unfair dismissal from 2 years to 6 months.[^s40][^s39]

## Am I an employee if I'm an apprentice?

Yes, in England. Section A5 of the Apprenticeships, Skills, Children and Learning Act 2009 says an approved English apprenticeship agreement is to be treated as a contract of service (an ordinary employment contract) and not as an old-style "contract of apprenticeship".[^s4] That matters because a contract of service gives you the full set of employee rights.

Acas confirms apprentices have the same employment rights as anyone with employee status; the only real difference is a separate minimum wage rate.[^s20] Time spent as an apprentice also counts towards continuous employment if you stay with the same employer afterwards, which affects things like redundancy pay later on.[^s20]

GOV.UK tells employers they must give apprentices the same conditions as other employees at a similar grade, including paid holidays, sick pay and any benefits such as childcare voucher schemes or mentoring.[^s2]

**GTOD take:** if anyone tells you "apprentices don't get X because they're trainees", that is wrong in England. Ask them to show you where it says so.

## What must be in my apprenticeship agreement?

Section A1 of the 2009 Act defines an approved English apprenticeship agreement as one where you work for an employer for reward in an occupation that has a published standard, and you receive training to reach that standard.[^s3]

GOV.UK says the signed apprenticeship agreement must state the skill, trade or occupation you are training for, the name of the apprenticeship standard, the start and end dates, and how much training you will get. Employers can write their own or use the government template.[^s1]

The Apprenticeships (Miscellaneous Provisions) Regulations 2017 add that the agreement must specify the amount of off-the-job training you will receive and the length of the practical period (the time you are expected to work and train).[^s5] The 2025 amendment regulations, in force from 1 August 2025, cut the minimum practical period from 12 months to 8 months.[^s6]

The 2026 to 2027 funding rules go further: the agreement must exist from the start and for the whole apprenticeship, must include your details, the standard and level, start and end dates (including end-point assessment), the practical period dates and the off-the-job training hours, must be extended if the programme is extended, and must be signed by both you and the employer (not the same person signing both).[^s7]

You should also get a separate contract of employment. GOV.UK says the contract should make clear what wage you will be paid and for what hours.[^s2] Every employee has a right to a written statement of main terms on day one, covering pay, hours, holiday, place of work, probation and notice, with a wider statement (pensions, grievance procedure and so on) within 2 months.[^s21]

## What is the training plan and who signs it?

The training plan is a separate document from the apprenticeship agreement. GOV.UK says it must be signed by you, your employer and your training provider, and must set out the planned content and schedule of training, what each of the three parties will do and offer, and how to resolve queries or complaints.[^s1]

Under the 2026 to 2027 funding rules the provider draws it up with input from you and the employer, its broad content must be agreed before any training is delivered, and it must include your name, job role and hours, the standard, planned off-the-job hours, the delivery model, and English and maths details.[^s7]

If you already have relevant qualifications or experience, the training can be shortened, but only if all three parties agree at the initial assessment.[^s1] Even then the practical period cannot go below 8 months and the off-the-job hours cannot go below 187.[^s7]

## How much paid holiday do apprentices get?

The same as any other worker. Almost everyone classed as a worker gets 5.6 weeks of paid holiday a year, which for someone on five days a week is 28 days.[^s10] The 28 days is a cap: working six days a week does not get you more.[^s10]

Part-timers get 5.6 weeks pro rata (for example 16.8 days on three days a week), and if you work irregular hours the leave builds up from the hours you have actually worked.[^s10] Bank holidays do not have to be given as extra paid leave; an employer can count them inside your 28 days.[^s10]

GOV.UK's apprentice page puts it as "at least 20 days paid holiday per year, plus bank holidays".[^s9] The two pages describe the same 28-day total in different ways. Check your contract to see whether bank holidays are on top of or inside your allowance.

Holiday continues to build up while you are doing off-the-job training or study, because that is working time.[^s20] Use the GOV.UK holiday entitlement calculator to check your exact figure if you start mid-year or work irregular hours.[^s11]

## Do apprentices get sick pay?

Yes. Statutory sick pay (SSP) is paid by your employer for up to 28 weeks.[^s12] As at September 2026 the rate is £123.25 a week or 80% of your average weekly earnings, whichever is lower; that rate applies for the tax year 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027.[^s14]

The rules changed on 6 April 2026 under the Employment Rights Act 2025. Acas summarises three changes: SSP is now paid from the first day of sickness (the old 3 waiting days are gone), the lower earnings limit has been removed so low-paid and part-time staff qualify, and the amount is the lower of the flat rate or 80% of average weekly earnings.[^s15] The 80% floor is what stops the flat rate exceeding a low earner's normal pay.

**GTOD take:** for a 16 to 18 year old apprentice on £8 an hour working 37.5 hours (£300 a week), 80% is £240, so you would get the flat £123.25. If you only work 15 hours (£120 a week), you would get £96 (80% of £120). The old rules would have paid nothing on £120 a week.

To qualify you must be classed as an employee (or paid through PAYE), have done some work for the employer, and tell them you are unwell before their deadline or within 7 days.[^s13][^s16] If you are off for more than 7 days in a row you need a fit note from a GP, nurse, pharmacist, physiotherapist or occupational therapist.[^s13] Your employer may have a more generous contractual sick pay scheme; the law says apprentices must get the same sick pay conditions as comparable employees.[^s2]

## How many hours can an apprentice work?

Adults cannot be made to work more than 48 hours a week on average, normally averaged over 17 weeks, unless they choose to opt out in writing.[^s17] If you are under 18 the limit is 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week, and you cannot opt out.[^s17]

Adult workers get one uninterrupted 20-minute break if the day is over 6 hours, 11 hours rest between working days, and 24 hours off each week (or 48 hours each fortnight).[^s18] Under-18s get a 30-minute break if they work more than 4.5 hours, 12 hours daily rest and 48 hours weekly rest, and these can only be skipped in an emergency where no adult is available, with compensatory rest within 3 weeks.[^s19]

The funding rules say working fewer than 30 hours a week or being on a zero-hours contract must not stop you completing an apprenticeship, but the provider must check your contract is long enough to finish the programme.[^s7]

Pay is covered in GTOD's separate minimum wage document. In short, as at September 2026 the apprentice rate is £8 an hour for under-19s and for 19-plus apprentices in their first year, and you must be paid for training time as well as work time.[^s14][^s2]

## How much off-the-job training am I entitled to?

Off-the-job training is training, other than on-the-job training, that you receive in your normal paid working hours (excluding overtime) to reach the apprenticeship standard.[^s8] It can be lectures, practical training, shadowing, mentoring, industry visits, learning support, writing assignments and revision. Onboarding, progress reviews, exams, standalone English and maths qualifications, and travel time do not count.[^s8]

The amount changed for apprentices starting on or after 1 August 2025. The old rule was a minimum of 20% of normal working hours (about 278 hours on a 12-month programme, based on 6 hours a week). The new rule is a specific minimum number of hours published for each individual standard; that number can be reduced for recognised prior learning but never below 187 hours.[^s8][^s7] The hours are no longer tied to the length of the programme, as long as the 8-month minimum is met.[^s8]

Two GOV.UK pages written for apprentices still say training must be "at least 20% of your normal working hours".[^s9] Acas says the same.[^s20] The funding rules and the off-the-job guidance are the more recent and authoritative documents, so trust the per-standard published minimum, and ask your provider to show you the figure for your standard. The rules apply to employers, employer-providers and main providers, and the 2026 to 2027 version is linked from the GOV.UK funding rules page.[^s45]

Off-the-job training must be in paid time. If any is scheduled outside your normal hours you must agree to it and be given time off in lieu or extra pay.[^s8] Every apprentice must be paid a lawful wage for time in work and in active learning.[^s7]

Your provider must plan some active learning at least every calendar month (or every 3 months on a block-release or front-loaded model). If nothing is planned in a month, you should be put on a formal break in learning rather than left drifting.[^s7]

## What if my employer goes bust or makes me redundant?

You have the same redundancy rights as any employee. Statutory redundancy pay needs 2 years of continuous service and is half a week's pay for each full year under 22, one week for each year aged 22 to 40, and one and a half weeks for each year from 41, up to 20 years.[^s23] For redundancies from 6 April 2026 a week's pay is capped at £751 and the maximum payout is £22,530; you have 6 months from the end of your job to claim.[^s23]

**GTOD take:** most 16 to 19 year old apprentices will not reach 2 years' service, so the money often matters less than keeping the apprenticeship alive. That is what the next bit is for.

If your employer is insolvent, you can claim from the government: up to 8 weeks of unpaid wages, up to 6 weeks of unpaid holiday, statutory notice pay (one week per year, up to 12 weeks) and any redundancy pay, all capped at £751 a week.[^s24]

For the apprenticeship itself, the Department for Education says that if your provider can keep delivering, funding continues for at least 12 weeks after redundancy so you have time to find a new employer.[^s22] If you have less than 6 months of training left, or have completed 75% or more, you can finish the apprenticeship with no new employer at all.[^s22] Contact DfE through its customer help portal with your redundancy notice and employment details.[^s22]

The funding rules confirm that a redundant apprentice counts as an "alternative English apprentice" and may continue training, and that English and maths funding up to level 2 continues after redundancy.[^s7] Once you find a new employer, they need to identify a provider to deliver the rest; you can use Find an apprenticeship to look for vacancies.[^s22] A new employer can also count time already served so that the 8-month minimum is not restarted, where 6 to 8 months remain.[^s7]

## What support can I get if I have a disability or health condition?

The Equality Act 2010 places a duty on employers to make reasonable adjustments where a rule, a physical feature or the lack of an aid puts a disabled person at a substantial disadvantage, and it says you cannot be made to pay for them.[^s26] GOV.UK confirms the duty covers apprentices and trainees, and gives examples: changing how work is done, physical changes like ramps, specialist equipment, working elsewhere, or a phased return after illness.[^s25]

Access to Work is a government grant for people aged 16 or over in paid work in England, Scotland or Wales (Northern Ireland has a separate scheme). An apprenticeship counts as paid work, and you can apply if you are about to start within 12 weeks.[^s27] It can pay for equipment and software, support workers, travel costs if you cannot use public transport, vehicle adaptations and workplace changes; it does not affect benefits and you do not pay it back.[^s28]

Your training provider can also claim learning support funding. Under the 2026 to 2027 funding rules this is a fixed £150 a month, paid in the months when reasonable adjustments are delivered and evidenced, with more available up to £19,000 a year and exceptional learning support above that. It is not taken from the employer's account and needs no employer contribution, and the need must be reviewed at least every 3 months.[^s7]

**GTOD take:** tell your provider at the initial assessment about any diagnosis, dyslexia, ADHD, autism, mental health condition or physical need. Learning support is claimed by the provider, so if they do not know, you get nothing.

## How do I complain about my employer, my training provider or the apprenticeship?

Start with whoever the problem is with. For your employer, Acas advises raising it informally first, then a formal written grievance if that fails or the issue is serious (for example harassment). Your employer must follow a full and fair procedure in line with the Acas Code and let you bring a colleague or union rep to the grievance meeting.[^s29] Your training plan must also say how queries and complaints are resolved.[^s1]

For your training provider, use their published complaints procedure and exhaust it, including any appeal, before going higher.[^s30]

Only then can you complain to the Department for Education. DfE accepts complaints from learners, parents, authorised representatives and employers (with your written permission), in writing by email or letter or via its customer help portal, to Customer Service Team, Department for Education, Cheylesmore House, Quinton Road, Coventry CV1 2WT.[^s30] DfE will look at the quality and management of training, delays or non-compliance with the provider's complaints procedure, assessment quality and equality issues, but not exam results or grades, employment issues, contract disputes, compensation claims or anything more than 12 months old.[^s30]

For general help, the Apprentice Support Centre helpline is 08000 150 400, email nationalhelpdesk@apprenticeships.gov.uk.[^s31] The same number is listed for the Apprenticeships and Skills helpdesk, open Monday to Friday 9am to 5pm, free from UK mobiles and landlines.[^s32]

If you want to take your employer to an employment tribunal (for example for unpaid wages, discrimination or unfair dismissal), you must first tell Acas and go through early conciliation.[^s35][^s33] The usual deadline is 3 months minus 1 day from the dismissal or the problem happening (6 months minus 1 day for statutory redundancy pay claims), and the clock pauses while conciliation runs.[^s34][^s33] Raising a grievance first does not extend the deadline.[^s35]

## Can an apprentice join a trade union?

Yes. You can join by contacting your workplace rep or by finding a union through the Certification Officer's list or the TUC's tool. Unions negotiate pay and conditions, raise members' concerns with employers and go with members to disciplinary and grievance meetings.[^s36] GOV.UK lists being dismissed for joining a trade union as a dismissal that is likely to be unfair.[^s37] Acas says union membership is a day-one right that applies during probation too.[^s39]

## Can I be sacked during my apprenticeship or probation?

You can be dismissed, but only fairly. The employer needs a valid reason it can justify, must act reasonably and consistently, investigate properly, and give you at least your contractual or statutory notice, whichever is longer.[^s38] Dismissal for joining a union, applying for maternity or paternity leave, whistleblowing or refusing to give up working time rights is likely to be unfair regardless of how long you have worked there.[^s37] Constructive dismissal is where the employer's conduct (like not paying you or letting harassment happen) forces you to leave.[^s37]

Probation has no legal status: employers can set any length, and your day-one rights (minimum wage, holiday, discrimination protection, whistleblowing, union membership) apply throughout.[^s39] Acas notes that apprentices are not guaranteed a job at the end; when a fixed-term apprenticeship ends without a job offer, that counts as a dismissal and the employer must follow a fair process.[^s20]

As at September 2026 you still normally need 2 years' service to claim ordinary unfair dismissal.[^s40] The Employment Rights Act 2025 reduces that to 6 months, and the government intends this to take effect from 1 January 2027.[^s40][^s39] The right to written reasons for dismissal also drops from 2 years to 6 months at the same time.[^s40]

**GTOD take:** the funding rules say the provider must check your contract is long enough to complete the apprenticeship, so a contract that quietly ends before the end date in your agreement is a red flag; raise it before you sign.[^s7]

## Are the rules different in Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland?

Employment law (holiday, sick pay, working hours, dismissal, discrimination) is the same across England, Scotland and Wales. The SSP rate and the April 2026 changes apply GB-wide.[^s14][^s15] Section 20 of the Equality Act extends to England, Wales and Scotland.[^s26] Access to Work covers England, Scotland and Wales, with a separate Northern Ireland system.[^s27]

Apprenticeship funding and structure are devolved, so everything above about agreements, training plans, the 8-month minimum, off-the-job hours, learning support and DfE complaints is England only.[^s7][^s30] Sections A1 and A5 of the 2009 Act apply to England (the "approved English apprenticeship").[^s3][^s4]

In Scotland, Skills Development Scotland runs Foundation, Modern and Graduate Apprenticeships; Modern Apprenticeships are open from age 16, last 1 to 5 years and run from SCQF level 4 to degree level, and you work for an employer and earn a wage.[^s41] In Wales you must be over 16, living in Wales and not in full-time education; you earn a wage with holiday pay, usually get one day a week to study, and programmes run 1 to 4 years across Foundation, Standard, Higher and Degree levels.[^s42] Northern Ireland apprenticeships are open to 16-year-olds and over with three levels to choose from, and information is on nidirect.[^s43]

Northern Ireland has its own employment law. The Employment Rights Act 2025 is a Great Britain act. nidirect's SSP page says SSP is paid by your employer for up to 28 weeks and points to GOV.UK for rates, but does not say whether the April 2026 day-one and 80% rules apply in NI.[^s44]
