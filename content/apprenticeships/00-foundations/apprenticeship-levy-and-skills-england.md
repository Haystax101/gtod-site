## In one line

As at September 2026 the tax large employers pay is still legally the apprenticeship levy (Part 6, Finance Act 2016); "Growth and Skills Levy" is the government's rebrand for a more flexible spending offer - apprenticeships, foundation apprenticeships and short "apprenticeship units" - and Skills England, an executive agency now under the DWP, has taken over most of the abolished IfATE's functions.

## Key facts

- The tax is still called the "apprenticeship levy" and is set out in Part 6 of the Finance Act 2016. That law has not been repealed.[^s2][^s3]
- It is charged at 0.5% of an employer's pay bill, with a £15,000 allowance, so in practice only employers with an annual pay bill over £3 million pay it, about 2% of UK employers. It is unchanged for the 2026-27 tax year.[^s3][^s15][^s16]
- "Growth and Skills Levy" is a policy and branding change to what levy funds can be spent on, not a new tax. The government says it is "transforming the apprenticeship levy into a new growth and skills levy".[^s4]
- Under the Growth and Skills Levy, levy funds can pay for apprenticeships, foundation apprenticeships, and new short modular "apprenticeship units".[^s1][^s4]
- Apprenticeship units are short courses of between 30 and 140 delivery hours, delivered over one to 16 weeks; the rules apply to units starting on or after 28 April 2026. Learners must be aged 19 or over.[^s4]
- From 1 January 2026, government and levy funding for Level 7 (master's-level) apprenticeships is no longer available for new starters aged 22 and over. Funding continues only for new starters aged 16 to 21, and for those aged 22 to 24 who are care leavers or have an EHCP. Anyone who started before 1 January 2026 is funded to completion.[^s5][^s12]
- From 1 August 2026, new levy funds entering an account expire after 12 months (previously 24 months); funds already in accounts before that date keep the 24-month expiry.[^s1]
- The automatic 10% government top-up on levy funds was removed from 1 August 2026.[^s1]
- The maximum share of levy funds an employer can transfer to other employers is 50% (up from 25%), covering both apprenticeships and apprenticeship units.[^s1]
- From 1 August 2026, for non-levy employers and for levy payers who exhaust their funds, apprentices aged 16 to 24 have 100% of training and assessment costs government-funded (previously this applied only up to age 21).[^s1]
- For apprentices aged 25 or over: non-levy employers pay 5% co-investment; levy payers who exhaust their funds pay 25% co-investment.[^s1]
- Foundation apprenticeships are paid Level 2 jobs-with-training for young people aged 16 to 21 (or 22 to 24 with an EHCP, care leavers, or prison leavers), lasting a minimum of 8 months.[^s6]
- Skills England was established as an executive agency on 2 June 2025 after IfATE was abolished; the legal basis is the Institute for Apprenticeships and Technical Education (Transfer of Functions etc) Act 2025.[^s7][^s8][^s9]
- Skills England moved from the Department for Education to the Department for Work and Pensions on 16 September 2025.[^s9][^s13]

## Has the apprenticeship levy been replaced by the Growth and Skills Levy?

Not as a tax. As at September 2026, the payroll tax large employers pay is still legally the apprenticeship levy, set out in Part 6 of the Finance Act 2016, which has not been repealed. Section 98 of that Act says: "A tax called apprenticeship levy is to be charged in accordance with this Part."[^s2] HMRC's own guidance still calls it "Apprenticeship Levy" and charges it at 0.5% of the pay bill.[^s3]

The "Growth and Skills Levy" is the government's new name for a more flexible offer of what that same levy money can be spent on. The official rules describe it as transforming the apprenticeship levy into a growth and skills levy that gives employers more flexibility and young people more opportunities.[^s4]

So: the tax, the levy account, and the way you pay it are the same. What changed from 2026 is the spending rules, meaning what the money can buy. It is a rebrand of the spending side, not a legal replacement of the tax.

## Who pays the apprenticeship levy and how much?

Employers with an annual pay bill over £3 million pay the apprenticeship levy. It is charged at 0.5% of the total pay bill, and every employer gets a £15,000 allowance to offset against it, which is why only employers with a pay bill over £3 million actually pay. It is collected every month through PAYE.[^s3] Because of the allowance, only about 2% of UK employers pay it.[^s15][^s16] These figures are unchanged for the 2026-27 tax year.[^s3]

You, as an apprentice, never pay the levy. It is a tax on your employer. If your employer is too small to pay the levy, the government funds your training instead.

## What can employers spend apprenticeship levy money on as at September 2026?

As at September 2026, money in an employer's apprenticeship service account can be spent on three things: apprenticeships (including foundation apprenticeships), the new short "apprenticeship units", and transfers to other employers.[^s1][^s4] Employers can transfer up to 50% of their funds to other businesses, and that 50% allowance covers both apprenticeships and apprenticeship units.[^s1]

Levy funds can only pay for training and assessment with approved providers. They cannot be used for wages, recruitment costs or travel.[^s1] Each apprenticeship sits in a funding band; the bands run from £1,500 to £27,000, and the band is the most the government will put towards that apprenticeship's training and assessment.[^s1][^s17]

## Can levy funds be used for short courses that aren't apprenticeships?

Yes, through "apprenticeship units". These are short, modular training courses that launched for new starts from 28 April 2026 under the Growth and Skills Levy.[^s18] Each unit lasts between 30 and 140 delivery hours and is delivered over one to 16 weeks. The learner must be aged 19 or over.[^s4] Units are targeted at critical skills and their content is drawn from existing apprenticeship standards, so they are elements of apprenticeships rather than entirely separate courses.[^s4]

> [!GAP]
> Some secondary sources (training-provider blogs) say employers can spend "up to 50% of their levy pot" on apprenticeship units. The primary GOV.UK rules do not state this. The only 50% figure in the official rules is the annual transfer allowance, the maximum an employer can transfer to other employers, which covers both apprenticeships and units.[^s1][^s4] We could not find a primary GOV.UK source setting a 50% cap on how much of an employer's own pot they can spend on units for their own staff. Treat the "50% for units" claim as unconfirmed.

**GTOD take:** apprenticeship units matter more to employers upskilling existing staff than to a 16 to 19 year old starting out. If you are applying for your first apprenticeship, the full apprenticeship or foundation apprenticeship is your route, not a unit. Units require you to be 19 or over and are short top-ups, not a standalone qualification pathway.

## Does the levy still fund Level 7 apprenticeships?

Only for young people. From 1 January 2026, government and levy funding for Level 7 (master's-level) apprenticeships stopped for new starters aged 22 and over. Funding continues for new starters aged 16 to 21, and for those aged 22 to 24 who are care leavers or have an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP). Anyone who started a Level 7 apprenticeship before 1 January 2026 keeps their funding through to completion.[^s5][^s12][^s19]

**GTOD take:** for a 16 to 19 year old this change is unlikely to block you. Level 7 is master's level, and you would normally reach it only after several years and lower-level qualifications first. If you start a Level 6 degree apprenticeship at 18 and would only reach Level 7 at around 22, that is the case where the cut could bite later, so check funding before committing to a long Level 6 then Level 7 pathway.[^s12]

## What is Skills England and what does it do now that it has absorbed IfATE?

Skills England is the national body for skills in England. It was established as an executive agency on 2 June 2025, after the Institute for Apprenticeships and Technical Education (IfATE) was formally closed; the House of Commons Library confirms that Skills England has taken over most of the functions IfATE used to carry out.[^s9][^s10] The legal basis is the Institute for Apprenticeships and Technical Education (Transfer of Functions etc) Act 2025, which abolished IfATE and transferred its functions to the Secretary of State, and most of those functions are now exercised by Skills England on the Secretary of State's behalf.[^s7][^s8]

Skills England was set up as an executive agency of the Department for Education, but it moved to the Department for Work and Pensions on 16 September 2025, along with responsibility for apprenticeships, adult further education, skills, training and careers. Responsibility for higher education, and for further education and skills for under-19s, stayed with the DfE.[^s9][^s13]

What Skills England now does:

- Maintains and develops occupational standards, the descriptions that apprenticeships and technical qualifications are built on.[^s7][^s10]
- Owns apprenticeship assessment plans, and reviews and withdraws standards and plans.[^s7]
- Recommends funding bands for apprenticeships; the Secretary of State for Work and Pensions makes the final decision, weighing the recommendation against impact and affordability.[^s11]
- Produces the skills needs assessment for the country. It has published skills reports including "Skills England: skills for growth and opportunity" (3 July 2025), "Assessment of priority skills to 2030" (12 August 2025) and "AI skills for the UK workforce" (29 October 2025).[^s7]

Note that T Level and some technical-qualification approval functions sit with DfE officials, not Skills England.[^s7]

## Does any of this change whether I can get an apprenticeship?

No. Apprenticeships still exist and are still funded for young people. If anything, the changes push more money towards under-25s. From 1 August 2026, apprentices aged 16 to 24 have 100% of their training and assessment funded by the government, whether or not their employer pays the levy (previously full funding stopped at 21).[^s1] New foundation apprenticeships give 16 to 21 year olds, and 22 to 24 year olds who are care leavers, prison leavers or have an EHCP, a paid Level 2 entry route lasting at least 8 months, with training fully government-funded.[^s6]

The main door that closed is Level 7 (master's-level) funding for new starters aged 22 and over, which is at the top of the ladder, not the bottom where you start.[^s5]

**GTOD take:** for a 16 to 19 year old applying now, the reforms are broadly good news, because more of the funding is aimed at your age group. Focus on finding a foundation apprenticeship or a Level 2 or 3 apprenticeship with an employer. The levy and Skills England changes are background plumbing that affects employers' budgets, not your eligibility to apply.

> [!GAP]
> Apprenticeship funding rules and rates are volatile and were changing repeatedly through 2026, with multiple published versions of the 2026-27 rules. Everything dated in this document is correct as at 2 September 2026 but should be re-checked against the live GOV.UK "Apprenticeship funding" page[^s1] and the current funding rules before any decision.
