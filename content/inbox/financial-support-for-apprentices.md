## In one line

An apprentice is an employee, not a student, so student money (loans, the 16 to 19 Bursary, Child Benefit for your parents) is closed, but worker support (Universal Credit, Access to Work, council tax disregard, discount cards) is open, and you pay no tuition fees.[^s1][^s6][^s32]

## Key facts

- As at September 2026, GOV.UK states you are not eligible for Student Finance England if you are doing a degree apprenticeship; no tuition fee loan and no maintenance loan.[^s1]
- Apprentices do not pay tuition fees: the training is paid for by the employer and the government, so there is no student debt.[^s32][^s15]
- The 16 to 19 Bursary Fund is for people at a publicly funded school or college, or on an unpaid training course; a paid apprenticeship is neither, so apprentices are outside it.[^s5][^s4]
- Parents cannot get Child Benefit for a child on an apprenticeship, except a Foundation Apprenticeship in Wales, as at September 2026.[^s6]
- Universal Credit is for people on a low income, out of work or unable to work, with no limit on hours worked, so a low-paid apprentice aged 18 or over can claim; it tapers by 55p for every £1 earned.[^s12][^s13][^s11]
- For council tax in England, an apprentice is disregarded (not counted) if the training leads to an Ofqual-recognised qualification and pay is no more than £195 a week; the student exemption itself is for full-time students on courses of at least 21 hours a week for at least a year.[^s7][^s9][^s8]
- The £1,000 payments for taking on a 16 to 18 year old go to the employer and the training provider, not to the apprentice.[^s15]
- Care leavers under 25 starting an apprenticeship in England on or after 1 August 2023 get a £3,000 bursary, paid as three £1,000 instalments through the training provider, tax free and ignored for Universal Credit.[^s19][^s21]
- Access to Work is open from age 16 to people starting or in paid work, and GOV.UK lists an apprenticeship as qualifying work; it covers England, Scotland and Wales but not Northern Ireland.[^s27]
- Learning support funding for apprentices with a learning difficulty or disability is fixed at £150 a month, paid to the provider, under the 2026 to 2027 funding rules.[^s17]
- The apprentice National Minimum Wage is £8.00 an hour from 1 April 2026, for under-19s and for 19 and overs in their first year.[^s3]
- For the 2026 to 2027 tax year, the personal allowance is £12,570 and employees pay 8% National Insurance on earnings between £242 and £967 a week.[^s29][^s30]
- Apprentices at any level, from Level 2 to degree Level 6/7, are eligible for a TOTUM card (the NUS discount card).[^s25]
- Rail: the 16-17 Saver costs £35 and gives 50% off with no minimum fare; the 16-25 Railcard costs £35 a year (£80 for 3 years) and gives a third off, with a £12 minimum fare on weekday mornings before 10am.[^s24][^s23]
- London: the Apprentice Oyster photocard gives 30% off adult Travelcards and Bus & Tram Pass season tickets, for London residents aged 18 or over in the first 12 months of an approved apprenticeship, £20 fee, no pay as you go discount.[^s22]

## Can apprentices get a student loan?

No. GOV.UK's student finance eligibility page says plainly that you are not eligible for student finance if you are doing a degree apprenticeship, as at September 2026.[^s1] That covers both the tuition fee loan and the maintenance loan. The same logic applies to lower-level apprenticeships: student finance only funds a listed set of higher education courses (degrees, HNCs, HNDs, foundation degrees and similar) at recognised institutions, and an apprenticeship is not one of them.[^s1]

Why: apprenticeship training in England is paid for by the employer (through the apprenticeship levy or a co-investment) and by the government, not by the apprentice.[^s15] Apprenticeships.gov.uk states that apprentices do not pay tuition fees, so there is no student debt.[^s32]

**GTOD take:** The flip side is that there is no maintenance loan either. Your wage is your only guaranteed income. Before you accept an offer, work out whether the salary covers rent, travel and food where the job actually is.

Note: GOV.UK says the student finance rules are changing for courses starting on or after 1 January 2027; apprentices remain outside the scheme.[^s1]

## Do apprentices pay tuition fees?

No, not in England. The government's apprentice-facing site states that apprentices do not pay tuition fees.[^s32] Costs are shared between the employer and government: from 1 August 2026, the government funds all training and assessment costs (up to the funding band maximum) for apprentices aged 16 to 24 at non-levy employers, and at levy-paying employers whose levy funds have run out; for apprentices aged 25 and over, non-levy employers pay 5% and government 95%.[^s15][^s18]

The apprentice is not a party to any of that. The 2026 to 2027 funding rules also require providers to withdraw any learner found to be unpaid under the National Minimum Wage Regulations, which underlines that you must be a paid employee throughout.[^s18]

## Can apprentices get the 16 to 19 Bursary Fund?

No, in practice. The 16 to 19 Bursary Fund eligibility page says you must be aged 16 and under 19 on 31 August 2026 and either study at a publicly funded school or college, or be on an unpaid training course.[^s5] The overview adds that the school or college must not be a university.[^s4] An apprenticeship is paid employment with an employer, so it fails the "unpaid" test.

The vulnerable bursary (up to £1,200) goes to young people in or leaving care, or receiving Income Support, Universal Credit in their own right, or DLA or PIP together with ESA or Universal Credit, but still only if they are at school, college or on unpaid training.[^s5][^s4]

## Will my parents lose Child Benefit if I start an apprenticeship?

Yes, in England, Scotland and Northern Ireland. GOV.UK states that a parent cannot get Child Benefit if their child is on an apprenticeship, unless it is a Foundation Apprenticeship in Wales.[^s6] Child Benefit continues past 16 only for "approved education" (full-time non-advanced study of more than 12 hours a week, such as A levels, T Levels or a pre-apprenticeship study programme) or "approved training", which is a short list of unpaid programmes: Foundation Apprenticeships and Traineeships in Wales, No One Left Behind in Scotland, and PEACEPLUS Youth Programme and Training for Success in Northern Ireland.[^s6]

Child Benefit also stops if the child is doing a course with an employer's agreement or training that is part of a job contract, which describes an apprenticeship exactly.[^s6]

**GTOD take:** This is the one that catches families out. Tell your parents before you sign, because it changes the household budget. If they also get Universal Credit or tax credits with a child element, they should ask DWP or HMRC how the change affects that too; I could not verify the child element rules here.

## Can apprentices claim Universal Credit?

Yes, if your income is low enough and you are 18 or over (16 and 17 year olds can only claim in limited situations, such as having a health condition with medical evidence or caring for someone on a disability benefit).[^s11] An apprenticeship is a job, and Universal Credit is for people who are on a low income, out of work or cannot work.[^s12] GOV.UK says there is no limit to how many hours you can work and still get Universal Credit.[^s13]

Your apprentice wages reduce the payment by 55p for every £1 earned; some claimants (those with children or a health condition limiting work) get a work allowance of £427 a month if they get housing support, or £710 a month if not, before the taper starts.[^s13] You must have £16,000 or less in savings and investments.[^s11]

The student restrictions do not apply to apprentices. The rules that limit claims for people "receiving education" cover courses at an education or training provider, and GOV.UK's guidance on Universal Credit and students does not mention apprenticeships at all.[^s14] As at September 2026, GOV.UK's Universal Credit eligibility page likewise says nothing about apprentices, which is consistent with apprentices being treated as ordinary workers.[^s11]

## Do apprentices pay council tax?

It depends on who else lives in the house. Council tax bills assume two adults; if everyone counted as an adult is "disregarded", the bill can fall by 25% or 50%, and GOV.UK lists people "on certain apprentice schemes" among those disregarded.[^s7]

For an apprentice to be disregarded in England, the employer must give a declaration that the training leads to a qualification accredited by a body recognised by Ofqual (or the Scottish equivalent) and that the apprentice will not be paid more than £195 a week.[^s7] That £195 figure comes from the Council Tax (Discount Disregards) Order 1992, which defines an apprentice as someone employed to learn a trade or profession, on a programme leading to a recognised qualification, on a salary or allowance of no more than £195 a week in total.[^s9] The power to disregard "apprentices" alongside students sits in Schedule 1 of the Local Government Finance Act 1992.[^s10]

**GTOD take:** At £8.00 an hour, 24 hours a week already takes you past £195, and a full-time 37.5 hour week at £8.00 is £300.[^s3] So most apprentices earn too much to be disregarded, and the disregard mainly helps very part-time or very low-paid apprentices. Anyone under 18 is disregarded regardless, and so is an 18 or 19 year old still in full-time education.[^s7]

The student exemption does not apply. Households where everyone is a full-time student pay no council tax, but "full-time student" means a course lasting at least a year with at least 21 hours of study a week (or, for under-20s doing up to A level standard, at least three months and 12 hours a week).[^s8] A degree apprentice is employed, with off-the-job training of at least 20% of working hours, and does not get a student certificate from a university in the way a full-time student does.[^s2] **GTOD take:** Treat yourself as a working adult for council tax unless your council tells you otherwise; ask your council in writing if you think the £195 apprentice disregard fits.

## Are apprentices eligible for the NUS card (TOTUM)?

Yes. TOTUM, the NUS discount card, says TOTUM and TOTUM+ are available to all UK apprentices at Intermediate (Level 2), Advanced (Level 3), Higher (Level 4/5) and Degree (Level 6/7) level.[^s25] The free TOTUM tier is digital only; TOTUM+ is paid and includes a physical card usable as proof of age; prices were not stated on the eligibility page as at September 2026.[^s25]

> [!GAP]
> The older "Apprentice Extra" card (the NUS Apprentice Extra scheme) appears to have been folded into TOTUM, but apprenticeextra.co.uk could not be opened (blocked by robots.txt) so I cannot confirm whether the separate site or card still exists, or its price. Ask your training provider, which is usually how apprentices sign up.

## What travel discounts can apprentices get?

Rail, England, Scotland and Wales:

- 16-17 Saver: £35 for a year, 50% off Standard Anytime, Off-Peak, Advance and Season tickets, no time restrictions and no minimum fare. Eligibility is by age only, so apprentices qualify; cards bought from 17 August 2026 last a full year even after your 18th birthday.[^s24]
- 16-25 Railcard: £35 for one year or £80 for three, a third off most fares. Eligibility is by age (buy before you turn 26), so apprentices qualify; there is a £12 minimum fare on weekdays between 4.30am and 9.59am, which hits commuters.[^s23]

London (Transport for London):

- Apprentice Oyster photocard: 30% off adult-rate Travelcards and Bus & Tram Pass season tickets. You must be 18 or over, live in a London borough, be in the first 12 months of an apprenticeship that is an approved standard (or SASE compliant) and enrolled for at least 12 months with an eligible provider. There is a £20 fee, the card expires after 12 months, it gives no pay as you go discount, and it becomes invalid if you leave or change apprenticeship.[^s22]

**GTOD take:** Compare the 16-25 Railcard against the 16-17 Saver if you are 17: the Saver's 50% with no morning minimum fare beats the Railcard for daily commuting, but it stops at 18. For a 16 or 17 year old apprentice in London, ask TfL about the 16+ Zip Oyster photocard; I could not verify its rules this session.

> [!GAP]
> Local council schemes (for example subsidised bus passes for apprentices) vary by area and I did not verify any. TfL's 16+ Zip Oyster page could not be opened (rate limited). Check your local council and the TfL site directly.

## Who gets the £1,000 for taking on a 16 to 18 year old apprentice?

The employer and the training provider, not you. GOV.UK's apprenticeship funding page, updated 29 July 2026, says both the employer and the training provider get an extra £1,000 for each apprentice who is aged 16 to 18, or aged 19 to 24 with an education, health and care plan or who has been in local authority care.[^s15] The employer's £1,000 is paid in two equal instalments on dates set out in the funding rules.[^s15]

The 2026 to 2027 funding rules (version 3, published 29 July 2026, in force from 1 August 2026) also add a separate "apprenticeship hiring payment" of £2,000 for non-levy employers who take on a 16 to 24 year old who started with them within the previous three months, from October 2026, paid in two instalments starting after 90 days.[^s18][^s16] Again this goes to the employer.

**GTOD take:** These payments are a reason small employers say yes to younger applicants. Mention your age band when you apply to a small company; it makes you cheaper to hire.

> [!GAP]
> The exact instalment dates for the £1,000 employer payment (commonly 90 days and 365 days in earlier years) are in the 2026 to 2027 funding rules PDF, which I could not read in full. Confirm from paragraphs 125 to 141 of version 3.

## What is the care leaver bursary for apprentices?

£3,000, paid to you. If you start an apprenticeship in England on or after 1 August 2023, are under 25 at the start, have not had this bursary before, and meet the legal definition of a care leaver (an eligible child, relevant child or former relevant child, in broad terms someone looked after by a UK local authority for at least 13 weeks since age 14), you get £3,000 paid as three instalments of £1,000 across the first year.[^s19][^s21] Apprentices who started before 1 August 2023 got a single £1,000.[^s19]

The Department pays your training provider, which must pass each instalment on to you within 30 days.[^s19] The bursary is tax free, attracts no National Insurance, and is not treated as income for Universal Credit.[^s19][^s21] You claim through your training provider with written confirmation of care status from your local authority; the funding rules require the provider to give every apprentice aged 24 or under the chance to declare eligibility.[^s21][^s17] The apprentices.gov.uk page says you do not have to tell the provider details about your time in care.[^s20]

Sources disagree on timing. Apprenticeships.gov.uk describes the instalments as at 3 months, 6 months and within 12 months of starting.[^s20] The DfE help centre article (updated 18 December 2025) says 60, 120 and 300 days after the start date, with the third paid earlier for foundation or short apprenticeships starting after 1 August 2025.[^s21] **GTOD take:** Trust the help centre article; it is the operational guidance providers follow and is more recent. Responsibility for the bursary transferred to the Department for Work and Pensions on 1 April 2026.[^s19]

## What support is there for disabled apprentices?

Three things, none of which is means-tested against your wage.

Access to Work: a government grant for people aged 16 or over with a physical or mental health condition or disability who are in, or about to start within 12 weeks, paid work; GOV.UK lists an apprenticeship as qualifying work, alongside work trials and internships.[^s27] It can pay for specialist equipment and software, a support worker or BSL interpreter, travel to work when you cannot use public transport, and vehicle or workplace adaptations, but not adjustments the employer must legally make anyway.[^s26] It is available in England, Scotland and Wales, not Northern Ireland, and mental health support through Access to Work can be claimed only once.[^s27] Apprenticeships.gov.uk points apprentices to it for reasonable adjustments.[^s33]

Learning support: under the 2026 to 2027 funding rules, a provider can claim learning support funding at a fixed £150 a month for an apprentice who needs reasonable adjustments, with higher costs claimable up to £19,000 a year.[^s17] This is paid to the provider to fund your support, not to you.

Disability benefits: GOV.UK notes that disabled apprentices may be able to claim benefits.[^s2]

> I could not open the GOV.UK PIP eligibility page (rate limited), so I cannot confirm here that PIP is payable regardless of work or earnings. That is the standard rule but it needs a citation before Charge states it.

## Does the NHS Learning Support Fund cover apprentices?

No. The NHS Learning Support Fund pays a £5,000 a year training grant (plus other allowances) to students on listed pre-registration healthcare degrees such as nursing, midwifery, physiotherapy and paramedic science.[^s28] To qualify you must be eligible for tuition fee and maintenance support from the Student Loans Company through Student Finance England, Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland.[^s28] A nursing or healthcare degree apprentice is not eligible for student finance, so cannot meet that condition.[^s1]

> [!GAP]
> The NHSBSA page I opened does not mention apprentices by name; the exclusion follows from the student finance condition. The NHSBSA eligibility sub-page returned a 404. A direct NHSBSA statement on degree apprentices would make this airtight.

## Do apprentices pay tax and National Insurance?

Yes, like any employee, once you earn enough. For the tax year 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027 the standard personal allowance is £12,570; income between £12,571 and £50,270 is taxed at 20%.[^s29] You pay National Insurance from age 16 if you earn more than £242 a week from one job; employees pay 8% on earnings between £242 and £967 a week (£1,048 to £4,189 a month) and 2% above that.[^s31][^s30]

**GTOD take:** A full-time apprentice at the £8.00 minimum for 37.5 hours earns £300 a week, about £15,600 a year, so you will pay a little tax and National Insurance even on the minimum apprentice rate.[^s3] At 30 hours a week you earn £240 and pay neither.

## What is the apprentice minimum wage?

From 1 April 2026 the apprentice rate is £8.00 an hour, the same as the under-18 rate; the 18 to 20 rate is £10.85 and the 21 and over rate is £12.71.[^s3] The apprentice rate applies only if you are under 19, or 19 and over and in the first year; after that you must get the normal rate for your age.[^s3][^s2] You can start an apprenticeship at 16, and GOV.UK describes it as being an employee earning a wage with holiday pay.[^s34] You are paid for your off-the-job training hours (at least 20% of your working hours) and get at least 20 days' paid holiday plus bank holidays.[^s2]

## Is it different in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland?

Partly. The National Minimum Wage rates, including the £8.00 apprentice rate, apply across the UK.[^s3] Child Benefit rules are UK-wide, with the one exception that a Foundation Apprenticeship in Wales counts as approved training, so Child Benefit continues for it.[^s6] Access to Work covers England, Scotland and Wales but not Northern Ireland.[^s27]

The £3,000 care leaver bursary, the £1,000 and £2,000 employer payments, the £150 a month learning support and the funding-band rules are England-only, because they come from the English apprenticeship funding rules.[^s17][^s15][^s18] The £195 a week council tax disregard figure is the England figure; Wales now differs.[^s9]
