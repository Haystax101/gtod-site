## In one line

Early-careers screening in the UK is high-volume and largely automated: applications per vacancy are at record levels, a test score below a threshold can end your application before any human reads it, and separately a growing number of employers run contextual and name-blind processes designed to change how your grades are read.[^e1][^e6][^e11][^e14]

## Key facts

- The Institute of Student Employers reported employers receiving an average of 140 applications per graduate vacancy in its 2025 Student Recruitment Survey, a level it describes as a record and which has held for two years, up from 86 per vacancy in 2022/23.[^e1][^e2]
- ISE respondents received just over one million applications for graduate jobs in 2024/25, about 4% up on the previous year.[^e1][^e2]
- ISE reported mean applications per hire of 101 for internships and 123 for undergraduate placements in the same survey, both up sharply year on year.[^e1]
- ISE reported apprenticeship hiring rising while graduate vacancies fell 8%, with 80% of employers expecting to maintain current apprenticeship levels.[^e3]
- The Cabinet Office publishes an algorithmic transparency record stating that Civil Service verbal and numerical test scores are compared to a decision rule, and an applicant scoring below a set value is classed as not having passed and is rejected.[^e6]
- The ICO states that a jobseeker's application may be rejected on a below-threshold score before a human recruiter reviews it, and that jobseekers have rights to explanation, challenge and human review.[^e10]
- The Civil Service committed in 2015 to name-blind recruitment for all roles below Senior Civil Service, with applications anonymised until interview, alongside commitments from large graduate and apprenticeship employers.[^e11][^e12]
- KPMG states that its measure of socio-economic background is parental occupation — the job of the highest earner in the household when you were 14 — following Bridge Group and Social Mobility Commission guidance, and has a 2030 target of 29% of partners from lower socio-economic backgrounds.[^e25][^e26]
- PwC published socio-economic background and disability pay gaps for the first time in its FY21 annual report, reporting 80% disclosure and 14% of respondents from a lower socio-economic background.[^e22][^e24]
- Over 400 employers have entered the Social Mobility Foundation's Employer Index, with 150 entrants in 2024; the Index benchmarks employer-led social mobility across eight areas.[^e19][^e21]

**GTOD take:** the useful thing about these numbers is not the fear, it is the arithmetic. At 140 applications per place, employers cannot read applications carefully at stage one, which is exactly why stage one is a test score and not an essay. Effort spent perfecting your personal statement is worth less than effort spent on the assessment that actually gates the process.

## How does automated sifting actually work?

The clearest documented example in the UK is the Civil Service, because the government publishes algorithmic transparency records for its own tools.

The Cabinet Office record for Civil Service verbal and numerical tests describes the mechanics plainly:[^e6]

1. The test uses a computer adaptive testing algorithm that chooses each question based on your performance and applies a stopping rule.
2. When the stopping rule triggers, the session ends and a final score is calculated.
3. The score is passed by API to the Civil Service Jobs system.
4. The score is compared against a decision rule. Below a set value, the application is classed as not passed and the applicant is rejected.

That is the whole sift: a number, a threshold, an automatic outcome. The government publishes a broader Ethics, Transparency and Accountability Framework for Automated Decision-Making that this sits under.[^e7]

Private-sector employers rarely publish this, but the ICO confirms the pattern exists across the market. Its guidance for jobseekers states that where a score is below a set threshold an application may be rejected before a human recruiter reviews it, and that many employers using automated recruitment are likely relying on solely automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects.[^e10]

The ICO's November 2024 audit of AI recruitment tool providers produced almost 300 recommendations. Findings included tools collecting more personal data than needed and retaining it indefinitely, tools allowing recruiters to filter on protected characteristics, and tools inferring gender or ethnicity from an application or from a name rather than asking the candidate. Every audited company accepted or partially accepted the recommendations.[^e8][^e9]

### What does this mean for you practically?

- The online test is usually the real first gate, not the application form.[^e6][^e10]
- Some employers combine the automated score with something else. EY states that it assesses suitability by considering online assessment scores alongside academic attainment at A level or equivalent.[^e28]
- You have documented rights: to be told automated decision-making is in use, to ask how the decision was reached, and to request human review of a solely automated decision.[^e10]
- Employers that use structured, standardised assessment argue it reduces bias precisely because every candidate is measured against identical criteria.[^e29]

## What is contextual recruitment and how is my data used?

Contextual recruitment means assessing your achievements against the circumstances in which you achieved them, rather than against an absolute bar. Three A levels from a school where the average is much lower is treated as a stronger signal than the same grades from a school where it is the norm.

The Solicitors Regulation Authority describes contextual recruitment as recruiters considering the context of a candidate's achievements, taking account of external factors that may have influenced grades or experience, and presents it as a route into law for candidates from less privileged backgrounds.[^e16] The SRA also advises employers to avoid unnecessary criteria and to be wary of using A level results and the type of school or university attended for shortlisting.[^e16][^e17] The Law Society publishes a fair recruitment toolkit covering the same ground.[^e18]

The best-known UK system is Rare Recruitment's Contextual Recruitment System, which Rare describes as using large datasets to identify candidates with the greatest potential, recalibrating the assessment of achievements to account for challenging circumstances, and retraining recruiters to counteract unconscious bias.[^e14][^e15]

> [!GAP]
> We could not verify from Rare's own documentation the specific list of data fields the Contextual Recruitment System uses, how each is weighted, or which employers currently use it, because the research session's search budget ran out before that could be confirmed on Rare's domain. Widely repeated claims about specific indicators (school average performance, free school meal eligibility, time in care, refugee status, POLAR participation quintiles) should be checked against Rare's own published material before being relied on. **GTOD take:** if an application form asks about your school's performance, free school meals, whether you were the first in your family to go to university, or your parents' jobs when you were 14, answer it accurately. These questions almost always exist to help you, they are usually separated from the assessing panel, and leaving them blank removes context that would otherwise have counted in your favour. If you are unsure, the employer's privacy notice has to tell you how the data is used — read it and then answer.

## What is name-blind recruitment and does it apply to apprenticeships?

Name-blind (or anonymised) recruitment means removing identifying details from applications before they are sifted, so that decisions rest on what you wrote rather than who you appear to be.

In 2015 the UK government announced a name-blind recruitment commitment. The Civil Service committed to name-blind recruitment for all roles below Senior Civil Service level, with applications anonymised until the interview stage.[^e11][^e12] The same announcement named large employers committing to name-blind applications for graduate and apprenticeship level roles, including KPMG, HSBC, Deloitte, Virgin Money, the BBC, the NHS, learndirect and local government.[^e12]

In practice, name-blind means you should:

- Remove your name, address, age, school name and other identifiers from any document you upload where the employer asks you to.[^e11][^e13]
- Expect anonymisation to end at interview — it is a sift-stage control, not a permanent one.[^e11]
- Not assume it removes all bias. Published critique notes that other signals such as work history and university attended survive anonymisation.[^e16]

> [!GAP]
> The 2015 government name-blind commitment is more than a decade old and we could not verify, within this research session, which of those named employers still operate name-blind sifting today or how it is applied to apprenticeship routes specifically. Check the employer's current process page.

## How do employers measure and target socio-economic background?

This matters to an apprenticeship applicant for a concrete reason: several large employers now have published targets that make hiring people from lower socio-economic backgrounds a measured objective, and apprenticeship routes are the main way they meet them.

**The measure.** KPMG states that the most robust measure of socio-economic background is parental occupation — the job held by the highest earner in your household when you were 14 — and that it follows guidance issued by the Bridge Group and the Social Mobility Commission in 2021.[^e25][^e26] This is why application forms ask that oddly specific question.

**The targets and the data.**

| Employer | Published commitment or data point | Source |
|---|---|---|
| KPMG UK | Target of 29% of partners from lower socio-economic backgrounds by 2030; uses parental occupation at 14 as the measure | KPMG[^e25][^e26] |
| KPMG UK | Its research found people from the lowest socio-economic backgrounds took on average 19% longer to progress to the next grade, the strongest effect of any characteristic studied | KPMG[^e25] |
| PwC UK | Published socio-economic background and disability pay gaps for the first time in its FY21 annual report; 80% disclosure rate, 14% of respondents from a lower socio-economic background | PwC[^e22][^e24] |
| PwC UK | Published targets in 2023 for representation from lower socio-economic backgrounds by grade up to 2030 | PwC[^e22][^e23] |
| PwC UK | Publishes salaries on school leaver apprentice programme job descriptions, and allows new joiners to advance up to £1,000 of their first month's salary | PwC[^e23] |
| PwC UK | Ranked first for social mobility in the Social Mobility Foundation Employer Index in consecutive years | PwC[^e23] |

**GTOD take:** the £1,000 salary advance is the kind of detail that decides whether an apprenticeship is actually doable for someone who cannot float a month of travel costs, and it is buried in a press release rather than on the application page. If money at the start is a problem for you, ask the recruiter directly whether the employer offers a salary advance, a travel loan or a starter payment. Some do and do not advertise it.

## What is the Social Mobility Employer Index and how should I use it?

The Social Mobility Foundation runs an annual Employer Index that benchmarks and ranks employers on employer-led social mobility across eight areas.[^e19][^e20] More than 400 employers have entered over its lifetime, with 150 entrants in 2024, including growth from the creative sector (up 43%) and the tech sector (up 60%).[^e21]

Practical use for an apprenticeship applicant: the Index is one of the few independent, published signals of whether an employer's social mobility work is real or is marketing. An employer that enters, publishes its ranking and moves up over time is measurably doing something.

> [!GAP]
> We could not verify the specific eight areas the Employer Index assesses, or whether apprenticeship provision is scored separately within it, before the research session's search budget ran out. The Social Mobility Foundation's own Employer Index pages are the place to check.

## What should I actually take away from all this?

1. **Assume the first stage is automated and treat the test as the real gate.**[^e6][^e10]
2. **Fill in the contextual and diversity questions accurately.** They are usually separated from assessors and exist to add context that helps you.[^e16]
3. **Anonymise uploads when asked to.** Name-blind sifting only works if you do your half of it.[^e11][^e13]
4. **Use published social mobility data to choose where to apply**, not just to feel reassured — an employer with a 2030 target and a published gap is an employer with a reason to hire you.[^e22][^e25]
5. **Ask about money at the start.** Salary advances, travel support and expenses exist at some employers and are rarely advertised.[^e23]
6. **Know your rights on automated decisions.** You can ask how a decision was reached and request human review.[^e10]
7. **Apply to more places than feels reasonable.** At 140 applications per graduate vacancy, single-employer strategies fail on arithmetic, not on merit.[^e1]
