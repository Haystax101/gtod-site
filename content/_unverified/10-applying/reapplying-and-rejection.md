## In one line

A degree apprenticeship rejection is now the statistically normal outcome
rather than a verdict on you, most employers let you reapply after a stated
waiting period, and the strongest response is to keep applying elsewhere in the
same cycle rather than waiting a year.

## Key facts

- **Competition for level 6 apprenticeships has roughly quadrupled in two
  years.** BBC analysis of Freedom of Information data from the Find an
  apprenticeship service put it at **11.3 applications per level 6 place in the
  current academic year, up from 2.8 in 2023/24** (as reported, accessed 2
  September 2026).[^s8][^s9]
- Over the same period applicants through the government platform rose from
  around 8,100 to around 21,800, while advertised positions fell from around
  7,300 to around 3,700.[^s8][^s9]
- Across all apprenticeships at levels 4, 5 and 6 the figure was **10.6
  applications per position, up from 3.6**.[^s8][^s9]
- **There is generally no cap on how many apprenticeships you can apply for**,
  and because apprenticeships are not tied to an annual admissions cycle you
  can apply whenever vacancies exist.[^s5][^s16]
- **Individual employers do set reapplication waiting periods.** EY's published
  candidate guidance describes a waiting period before reapplying after an
  unsuccessful application, with a shorter period for apprenticeship vacancies
  than for graduate and undergraduate ones.[^s2][^s3]
- **Foundation apprenticeships** are paid level 2 roles in England for people
  aged 16 to 21 (or 22 to 24 with an EHC plan, in local authority care, or with
  a prison record), available from 1 August 2025, with a minimum duration of
  eight months.[^s10][^s11][^s12]

## How competitive are apprenticeships actually? (Read this before blaming yourself.)

The single most useful thing to understand after a rejection is the base rate.

BBC analysis of Freedom of Information data from the government's Find an
apprenticeship service in England found that in the current academic year there
were **11.3 applications for every level 6 apprenticeship place**, compared
with 2.8 in 2023/24.[^s8][^s9] The rise came from both directions at once:
applicants roughly tripled, from about 8,100 to about 21,800, while the number
of advertised positions nearly halved, from about 7,300 to about
3,700.[^s8][^s9]

The reporting attributes the squeeze to rising scrutiny of university costs
pushing demand up, and to employers cutting back on hiring because of
employment costs and economic uncertainty.[^s8][^s9] Applicants told the BBC
they were applying to university as a backup because competition was so
fierce.[^s8]

**Two important caveats on that number:**

- It covers only apprenticeships advertised through Find an apprenticeship in
  England, because that is the service subject to FOI.[^s8] Schemes recruited
  solely through an employer's own site are not in it.
- Applications per place is not the same as a success rate. One person can
  submit many applications, so an individual's odds are not simply 1 in 11.

**GTOD take:** an 11-to-1 ratio means a strong candidate can be rejected from
several schemes and still be a strong candidate. Treat rejection as information
about the market, not about your worth. What it should change is your
*strategy* — apply to more places, earlier — not your self-assessment.

## Can I reapply to the same employer?

**Usually yes, but often not immediately.** There is no national rule. Each
employer sets its own policy, and the policies genuinely differ.

**A published worked example — EY.** EY's candidate guidance for its UK early
careers programmes describes a waiting period after an unsuccessful
application:

- For **apprenticeship** vacancies, candidates rejected at any stage are told
  they can apply again after a stated waiting period, described in EY's
  candidate Q&A as three months.[^s2]
- For **graduate and undergraduate** vacancies, the stated waiting period is
  six months from the date of the earlier application.[^s3]
- The guidance also refers to a limit on the number of applications per
  year.[^s3]

> [!GAP]
> EY's published statements are not fully consistent across the pages we found:
> some references give three months for apprenticeships and others six months
> from the first application, and we could not resolve which currently applies
> or confirm the exact per-year application limit without opening EY's own
> Early Careers FAQ page.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Anyone advising an EY applicant should
> read that FAQ directly rather than rely on this summary.

**What this tells you about employers generally:**

- Assume a waiting period exists until you have read otherwise.
- The waiting period usually runs from the date you were rejected, or from the
  date of your original application. Which one it is matters, so check.
- A waiting period does not stop you applying to a *different* employer
  tomorrow.
- Some employers restrict how many applications you can make per recruitment
  year, so applying to three of an employer's schemes at once can burn your
  allowance.

**GTOD take:** never withdraw from a process to "reapply better" without
checking the policy first. On some employers' systems, withdrawing starts the
same clock as a rejection, and you have then locked yourself out of a scheme
you were still live in.

> [!GAP]
> EY is the only UK employer whose apprenticeship reapplication policy we could
> verify as published in this session. Other large early careers employers
> almost certainly publish theirs too. Collecting five or six named policies
> into a table would make this section far more useful and is the highest-value

## How do I ask for feedback after a rejection?

**Be realistic first: most rejected applicants get no meaningful feedback,
especially at the early screening stages.** Automated sifts and online
assessments are typically not explained to candidates, and employers processing
thousands of applications rarely give individual reasons.

Where feedback is realistically available, it is after a **human** stage — a
video interview, an assessment centre, or a final interview.

**How to ask, based on published careers guidance:**[^s6][^s7]

1. **Reply to the rejection professionally.** TARGETjobs notes that responding
   courteously to a rejection, particularly at the final stage, can leave a
   positive impression with the recruiter that helps if you apply again
   later.[^s6]
2. **Ask forward, not backward.** Frame your question around what you could do
   better next time rather than what went wrong this time — guidance
   specifically recommends this framing.[^s7]
3. **Keep it short and polite**, and accept that a non-answer is a possible
   outcome.[^s7]
4. **Weigh the feedback rather than obeying it.** Recruiter opinions are
   subjective, and different employers can give you conflicting observations.
   Getting a second opinion on whether feedback is worth acting on is sensible
   guidance.[^s7]

**GTOD take:** a workable email is three sentences — thank them, say you would
value one thing to work on before you apply again, and say you remain
interested in the organisation. Send it within 48 hours to the named recruiter
if you have one, not to a generic careers inbox. The response rate is low. It
is still worth ten minutes.

## What are my actual alternatives after a rejection?

Ranked by how quickly they keep you moving, rather than by prestige.

### 1. Keep applying this cycle (almost always the right first move)

Apprenticeship windows are staggered across roughly September to
February, and vacancies are advertised year-round rather than in a single
admissions round.[^s5][^s16] A rejection in November does not end the year. See
`./application-timeline-and-deadlines.md`.

### 2. Apply at a lower level with the same employer or sector

A level 4 or level 5 higher apprenticeship is a genuine route in. Many employers
run progression from level 4 upward, and the entry bar is usually lower than
level 6. The BBC figures show competition at levels 4 to 6 combined was 10.6
applications per place, so this is not an easy option, but it is a different
pool.[^s8]

### 3. Foundation apprenticeship (England, if you are 16 to 21)

**Foundation apprenticeships are paid level 2 jobs with training**, introduced
for starts from 1 August 2025 in England.[^s10][^s12]

- Open to people aged 16 to 21 at the start of training, and to those aged 22
  to 24 who hold an EHC plan, are in the care of their local authority, or have
  been in prison.[^s10][^s12]
- Minimum duration of eight months, following the reduction of the minimum
  apprenticeship duration from twelve months to eight from 1 August
  2025.[^s11][^s12]
- Available across sectors including business administration, construction,
  digital, hospitality and health and social care.[^s10]
- No strict academic prerequisites are stated; if you do not already hold
  English or maths, the provider supports you to a level 1 or level 2
  functional skills qualification during training.[^s10]

**GTOD take:** for someone who did not get the grades or did not get an offer, a
foundation apprenticeship is a paid job with a qualification attached, which is
materially better than an unpaid year. It is not a degree apprenticeship and
should not be sold as one, but it puts you in employment and gives you a real
employer reference for next time.

> [!GAP]
> Scotland separately uses the term "Foundation Apprenticeship" for a different
> thing — a work-based qualification typically taken while still at school. We
> did not verify the Scottish definition, level or eligibility in this session.
> Do not conflate the two. Checking apprenticeships.scot would settle it.

### 4. Stay in education and reapply next cycle

- **A fourth year, a resit year, or an extra level 3 qualification** raises your
  grades if grades were the binding constraint.
- **College at level 3** if you did not achieve the entry qualifications.
- Reapplying with achieved rather than predicted grades removes the single
  biggest source of uncertainty in an apprenticeship offer.

### 5. A structured gap year, then reapply

Careers guidance is consistent that a reapplication needs to show you have
become a stronger candidate, and that a productive gap year can serve that
purpose — but only if it is deliberately planned rather than an extended
break.[^s6]

**GTOD take:** a gap year works for apprenticeship reapplication if it produces
something specific and datable: a full-time job with responsibility, a
qualification, a portfolio, or a piece of relevant work experience. It does not
work if the honest account of it is "I worked in a shop and applied again".
Decide the artefact before you decide the year.

### 6. Entry-level employment

An entry-level or administrative role with an employer who runs apprenticeships
gets you paid, gets you an employment reference, and in many organisations makes
you eligible to be put on an apprenticeship as an existing employee. Existing
staff are a real recruitment channel for apprenticeships, not just
school-leavers.[^s16]

> [!GAP]
> We could not verify what proportion of apprenticeship starts are existing
> employees rather than new external hires. DfE apprenticeship statistics[^s18]
> or the House of Commons Library briefing[^s20] may carry this. It would
> materially strengthen the advice above.

### 7. University, then a graduate scheme

This is a legitimate route to the same destination and should not be framed as
defeat. TARGETjobs makes the point that a rejection assesses you for a
particular role at a particular moment against a particular applicant pool, and
does not mean you will never work for that employer.[^s6] Many of the same
firms that run degree apprenticeships run graduate schemes, and you can apply
to those in your final year.

The trade-offs are real and worth stating plainly: you take on tuition and
maintenance debt and you start earning three years later, but you keep more
flexibility about what you specialise in and you are not tied to one employer
at 18.

**GTOD take:** if you hold a university offer and no apprenticeship offer on
results day, take the university place. You can reapply to apprenticeships in
your first term — some employers accept applications from first-year
undergraduates — and you can leave. What you cannot easily do is manufacture a
university place in October once you have declined it.

## What should I actually do in the week after a rejection?

1. **Reply to the rejection email politely** and ask for one piece of forward
   feedback if you reached a human stage.[^s6][^s7]
2. **Check that employer's reapplication policy** and diarise the date you
   become eligible again.[^s2][^s3]
3. **Submit two more applications elsewhere within seven days.** The
   application material is already written; the marginal cost is low.
4. **Log the rejection in your tracker** with the stage you reached, so you can
   see whether you are consistently failing at the same point. See
   `./application-tracking-and-organisation.md`.
5. **Check live vacancies** on Find an apprenticeship and the higher and degree
   listings, which refresh year-round.[^s14][^s15]

**GTOD take:** the pattern in your rejections matters more than any single one.
Rejected at online assessment every time means practise the tests. Rejected at
assessment centre every time means the problem is the group exercise or the
interview, which is a completely different fix. You can only see that pattern
if you wrote it down.
